## Direct answer

Chasing losses means increasing your stakes, extending your session, or playing faster specifically to "win back" money you've already lost — and it's one of the clearest warning signs of harmful play, not a normal recovery strategy. It happens because a loss triggers an urge to fix it immediately, and that urge gets mistaken for a plan. The fix isn't willpower in the moment; it's deciding your stopping point *before* a loss happens, because that's the only time you can decide it clearly.

## Why loss-chasing feels logical (and isn't)

Our [responsible gaming](/responsible-gaming/) page already flags this directly: "Never chase losses — increasing stakes to 'win back' a loss is one of the clearest warning signs of harmful play." That line is true, but it's short, and the reason people do it anyway deserves more explanation than a single bullet point.

After a loss, the brain doesn't experience it as "that money is gone." It experiences it as an open problem — a debt against your own sense of having been in control. Increasing your stake to win it back doesn't feel like a new gamble; it feels like *correcting* the last one. That's the trap: it's framed internally as damage control, not as additional risk, even though mathematically it's the opposite. A bigger bet after a loss doesn't reduce your risk — it increases what you stand to lose if the next hand also goes against you.

This is sometimes called the "sunk cost" pull, and it's not a sign of poor discipline specifically around gaming — it's a general feature of how people respond to loss in lots of situations. Rummy and Teen Patti don't create this instinct; they just give it a fast, repeatable outlet, because the next hand is always seconds away.

## What loss-chasing looks like in practice

It rarely looks as obvious as "I will now bet everything to win it back." It usually looks like smaller, easier-to-rationalize steps:

- Playing one more round "just to end on a better note," which turns into several more rounds.
- Raising your stake size specifically because the last hand or two went badly, not because of anything about the current hand.
- Switching from your planned session length to "I'll stop once I'm back to even," which has no fixed endpoint.
- Telling yourself the next hand is "due" to go your way — real-money card games don't work this way; each hand is its own outcome.
- Feeling a spike of urgency or irritation specifically after a loss, rather than calm, measured decision-making.

Any one of these on its own might be nothing. The pattern to watch for is doing it *specifically because of a preceding loss*, rather than because you'd planned to play that amount or that long regardless.

## Why it doesn't work as a strategy

Treat this plainly: there is no betting pattern, stake adjustment, or timing approach that turns a losing session into a winning one through persistence alone. Each hand's outcome doesn't depend on what happened in hands before it. Increasing your stake after a loss doesn't improve your odds on the next hand — it only increases the size of the swing, in either direction. The practical effect of loss-chasing is that a loss you could have afforded at your original stake becomes a loss you can't afford at an increased one.

## How to stop the pattern before it starts

1. **Set your stopping point before you play, not during.** As our [gaming budget guide](/blog/how-to-set-a-gaming-budget/) covers in more detail, a loss limit decided in advance is the single most effective defense here.
2. **Treat "I'll stop once I'm back to even" as a warning sign, not a plan.** A real stopping point is a fixed number or a fixed time, decided beforehand.
3. **Build in a pause after any loss that tempts you to raise your stake.** Step away from the app for a few minutes.
4. **Separate your gaming funds from the rest of your money**, so that "winning back" a loss never means pulling from funds set aside for something else.
5. **Keep a simple log of your sessions** — what you set out to spend, what you actually spent, and whether you stopped on time.
6. **If you notice the pattern repeating across multiple sessions, treat that as the signal to stop for longer than one day**, not just that day.

If you've noticed yourself chasing losses more than once, or if it's affected money you'd set aside for something else, that's a point to involve someone outside the game. ## FAQ

**Is chasing losses the same as bad luck?**
No. Bad luck is a losing hand. Chasing losses is the decision to increase your stake or keep playing specifically *because* of a previous loss, in an attempt to reverse it.

**Does increasing my bet after a loss improve my odds of winning it back?**
No — each hand is an independent outcome. A larger bet after a loss increases what's at stake on the next hand; it doesn't make that hand more likely to go your way.

**What should I do the moment I notice I'm chasing a loss?**
Stop the session there. Step away from the app for a few minutes before deciding anything else.
